How reliable these numbers are

    Method and sources

    • Households: Census Bureau American Community Survey, 2024 one-year microdata for Florida, with incomes and home values brought forward to 2026.
    • Tax law: 2026 Florida assessment and exemption rules (homestead exemptions, Save Our Homes and the 10% non-homestead cap, senior, veteran and disability exemptions), each tied to its statute in the model's rules file.
    • Checked against the property rolls: on 9.85 million residential parcels in the 2026 Department of Revenue rolls, the rules reproduce the official taxable value exactly for 99.4%. Modelled homestead tax averages within 1% of the rolls, and within 4% for every home-value band from $100,000 to $2 million.
    • Save Our Homes: how far each home's assessed value sits below market value is drawn from real homestead parcels in the same area and value range, matched to owners using how long they have lived there and the tax they report.
    • Rates: 2026 school rates from the state's required local effort; county rates as adopted where collected, otherwise estimated from Florida's rate-setting rules; 2026 city rates for 91 cities (from county property appraisers and city records), other cities at 2025 levels.
    • Income groups: tenths of all Florida households by 2026 household income, renters included, so the groups will not move when renters are added.
    • Margins of error: 90% intervals from the survey's 80 replicate weights.

    What drives these projections

    How to read the range

      Method and sources

      • Current law only. Florida's assessment rules, exemptions and rate limits as written for 2026, with the Save Our Homes cap following inflation and the indexed exemption amounts growing with it. No future law changes, including Amendment 3 on the November 2026 ballot.
      • Home values: the state Revenue Estimating Conference's August 2026 forecast for existing residential property through 2032, then easing to the Florida Economic Estimating Conference's sale-price path (about 2% a year) by 2036.
      • Incomes and households: Florida Economic Estimating Conference (July 2026) wage, income and household forecasts; Social Security and pensions grow with inflation (CBO).
      • Sales and resets: homes change hands about 5.5% a year (homesteads), less often the larger their Save Our Homes savings, calibrated to the state's forecast of homestead assessment differences; deaths of owners living without a spouse are part of this. 14% of homestead sellers carry over savings (portability).
      • Tax rates: each county, city and school district's yearly rate change is drawn from what Florida governments of the same kind did in years with similar growth (1996-2025), within the 2026 law's limit of 110% of the rolled-back rate.
      • New owners: owner households grow with Florida's household forecast; new owners live in new homes taxed on full value, placed where new construction happens.
      • Population: owners shift toward older ages following Florida's official population projections by age.

      How to read this

      • Revenue lost is property tax on all property, homes, rentals, second homes and businesses, compared with current law, with every government's tax rate where it would be under current law (how the state's economists score tax changes). Tax on property other than owner-occupied homes is estimated from each county's 2026 tax roll and average rates. "After governments respond" nets out the rate increases governments have made in similar years.
      • Two versions of every result. "Governments respond": counties, cities and school districts set rates the way Florida governments have in comparable years since the 1990s, within the 2026 limit of 110% of the rolled-back rate; when a policy shrinks their tax base, some raise rates, so owners who don't benefit can pay more. "No response": rates follow exactly the path they would under current law, showing the policy's direct effect. Tick "Localities replace lost revenue" for a third version: every county, city and school district resets its rate so it raises the same revenue as under current law, so a tax cut for some owners is paid for by higher rates on everyone else in that place (school districts keep the state-set required rate and adjust their local part). The slider sets how much of the lost revenue the state pays back to local governments, as Maine does for its homestead exemption; local rates rise only to cover the rest. With this ticked, section 4 lists every county, city and school district's rate.
      • One change at a time. Each slider changes one number (or a set that moves together), with everything else at current law. The listed policies combine changes. Free combinations need a live engine, planned for later.
      • Revenue replaced is a check, not a forecast. It shows who would pay if no local government cut spending. Each government's tax base follows its homesteads in the model and its other property from the 2026 rolls (including the non-homestead assessment caps). It ignores legal rate limits (10 mills for counties, cities and schools' local part), which large policies would exceed in some places.
      • Big policies are outside history. Rate responses are drawn from ordinary years; for policies that remove most of a government's homestead base, treat the "respond" figures as indicative only.
      • Owners only. Renters' share through rent isn't modelled yet. "About the same" means within $10 a year.

      Method

      • Both runs use the same households, economy (central forecast) and random draws, so differences come from the policy.
      • Changes start in tax year 2027. Indexed amounts keep growing with inflation from the new value.
      • Income-tested exemptions are re-tested each year; newly eligible households claim at today's rates where their county or city offers the exemption.
      • Income deciles are 2026 groups of all Florida households.

      What Amendment 3 does

      The Legislature's proposal (CS/HJR 1-F, 2026), titled "Save Our Homes from Excessive Property Taxes", changes the State Constitution in four ways. The model covers the first three.

      1 · A larger homestead exemption

      For county, city and special district taxes, the first $150,000 of a homestead's assessed value is exempt in 2027 and the first $250,000 from 2028, rising with inflation from 2029. Today it is $25,000 plus a second exemption worth up to about $26,000. School taxes keep the $25,000 exemption.

      2 · New residents wait

      People who were not Florida residents on December 31, 2026 get the first $50,000 exempt (rising with inflation from 2028) until their fifth year with the exemption. From 2030 a county or city can shorten the wait by a two-thirds vote. A similar wait was struck down by the Florida Supreme Court in 1982, so this page also shows the amendment without it.

      3 · A 5% cap on other property

      For non-school taxes, assessments of non-homestead homes, rentals, commercial and other property can rise at most 5% a year, down from 10%. Most of this goes to rentals and businesses, which the model counts as local tax base, not as households.

      4 · Limits on spending (not modelled)

      Counties and cities could use property tax only for public safety, education, infrastructure, natural resources and flood control, bonds and debt, employee retirement, and the operations of county officers, commissions and city governments. The last category is broad, so the model assumes no change in what they levy.
