    What Amendment 3 does

    The Legislature's proposal (CS/HJR 1-F) is on the November 3, 2026 ballot, needs 60% of the vote, and would take effect January 1, 2027.

    • A larger homestead exemption. For county, city and special district taxes, the first $150,000 of a homestead's assessed value is exempt in 2027 and the first $250,000 from 2028, rising with inflation. Today it is about $51,000. School taxes keep the $25,000 exemption.
    • New residents wait. People who were not Florida residents on December 31, 2026 get $50,000 until their fifth year with the exemption. A similar wait was struck down by the Florida Supreme Court in 1982, hence the switch above.
    • A 5% assessment cap for rentals, second homes and businesses. Their assessed value for non-school taxes can rise at most 5% a year, down from 10%.
    • Limits on spending (not modelled). Counties and cities could use property tax only for listed purposes; the list includes their general operations, so the model assumes no change in what they levy.

    How it works

    From survey households to tax bills

    The model applies Florida's property tax law to a representative sample of the state's owner households, then compares each policy with current law on the same households, economy and random draws, so the differences come from the policy alone.

    1. HouseholdsCensus survey of Florida households (2024), brought forward to 2026.
    2. Matched to the rollsHomestead status, home values, Save Our Homes discounts and exemptions drawn so every area matches the 2026 parcel rolls.
    3. Tax rulesAssessment caps and exemptions from the statutes, in a rules file with a citation for each number.
    4. Local tax ratesEach owner's real tax district: school, county, city and special district rates.
    5. Projections and policiesYear by year to 2036: values, sales, new homes, and how governments set rates; then each policy against current law.

      Assumptions

      Key assumptions

      • Three ways local governments can respond. Rates unchanged (or "no response"): every rate follows its current-law path; this is how the state's economists score tax changes. Governments respond: rates change the way Florida counties, cities and school districts changed them in comparable years since the 1990s, within the limit of 110% of the rolled-back rate. Localities replace lost revenue: each government resets its rate to raise what it would under current law, a check rather than a forecast. It respects the Constitution's 10-mill cap on county general and city levies (what a capped government can't raise stays lost); county taxing units and special districts rise in full. Options add state reimbursement or the state's usual offset for 29 small, rural counties (s. 218.125).
      • People respond to bigger exemptions. Some owners who could claim the homestead exemption but don't start claiming (20% of them at a $2,500-a-year gain, phased in over two years, from Texas's 2023 increase), and some out-of-state owners of second homes make Florida their legal residence (5% at that gain). Florida's 2008 increase showed no visible jump, so these are assumptions; the state's estimates leave them out. The Amendment 3 page shows the revenue lost from 10% to 40% of non-claimers and 2% to 10% of second-home owners.
      • New residents (Amendment 3): the Census share of recent buyers who came from another state or abroad (23%, more at older ages), plus newcomers who rent first (20% of arriving renters buy within five years).
      • Values and incomes: the Revenue Estimating Conference's August 2026 forecasts through 2032, then easing to about 2% a year by 2036; incomes and households from the state's July 2026 forecasts; inflation from the Congressional Budget Office.
      • Sales and new homes: homesteads change hands about 5.5% a year, less often the bigger their Save Our Homes savings, and 14% of sellers carry savings to their next home. New owners live in new homes taxed at full value, placed where construction happens. Owners shift older with the state's population projections.
      • Other property (rentals, second homes, businesses): each county's tax base from the 2026 rolls, with the assessment caps applied in bands, grown with the state's forecasts and taxed at the county's average rates.
      • Income groups are tenths of all Florida households by 2026 income, renters included.
        Limits

        What the model doesn't cover

        • Renters. What renters pay through rent isn't modelled, so renters appear to pay nothing; nor are mobile-home owners on rented lots.
        • Fees. Non-ad valorem assessments (fire, waste, stormwater) are on tax bills but aren't property tax, and are left out.
        • Business property counts toward revenue and tax bases but not as households.
        • Other responses: home prices adjusting to tax changes, people moving because of taxes, builders responding, and governments cutting spending.
        • Household details are estimates. Homestead status, Save Our Homes discount and tax district are right for groups, not for any single household. Small rural counties share survey areas, so county results are reliable only for larger counties.
        • Income is one year's income, not wealth: retirees with paid-off homes and little income look poorer than they are.
        • Some 2026 rates are 2025 rates: cities without a collected 2026 rate, and special districts.
        • Amendment 3: the limits on how counties and cities spend property tax, and local options (raising the exemption further, shortening the wait for new residents from 2030), are not modelled.
